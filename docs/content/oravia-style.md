# Craft & Style
This guide is meant for advanced users. There are many things you can do with Oravia, perhaps uniquely so, but they may be confusing if you approach it as a beginner. Proceed with care.

Oravia's expressiveness comes frmo multiple layers of both freedom and control: it allows you to be creative and flexible in your choices, but also gives you a toolkit to play with. When it comes to grammar, we have things like flexible word order, evidentiality, the emphasis marker, and much more. In this style guide, we introduce things like register, speech-act force, emotional texture, cluster connotation, sarcasm, scope, contrast and poetic sound-symbolism.

Much of its expressiveness comes from the small system being reusable across grammar, discourse, style, and word formation. In sum, in line with the guiding principle of Oravia, it can express a wide range of flavors with a light load.

Such a focus on expression is not common among auxlangs. But I strongly believe that to be a full language, it must be able to communicate and reflect the human experience.

**Most stylistic choices are either optional or can be derived naturally through use**. If you'd like to check the foundations of the grammar and start speaking, check the Core Grammar or the Core Course.

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>0. Glossary of common terms</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p><em>Vocabulary</em> - the bulk of the language, ~800 words, almost all composed of (sub)cluster + root (e.g., ANE + LEM = remain).</p>
<p><em>Cluster</em> - the first two letters and/or syllable of a word, which indicates its noun class (there are 48 clusters). Not all words belong to a cluster but almost all do (e.g., AN = movement). The exceptions are typically pronouns, prepositions, and numbers.</p>
<p><em>Subcluster</em> - subdivision of a cluster, typically indicated by the third letter. Clusters have between 0-4 subclusters (e.g., ANE = static movement. There is also ANI = movement toward, and ANO = movement away).</p>
<p><em>Root</em> - typically the rest of the word after subtracting the cluster, creating cross-cluster associations (e.g., LEM = remain, like BEILEM = vehicle + remain = station).</p>
<p><em>Building Blocks</em> - all the clusters, subclusters and roots, which form the syllable-meaning associations of the language (e.g., ANE = static movement, ANI = movement toward, ANO = movement away, LEM = remain, BEI = vehicle).</p>
<p><em>Marker</em> - they indicate the syntactic role of a word in a sentence by introducing blocks (e.g., [SUBJECT my mom and I] [VERB give freely] [DIRECT OBJECT homemade food] [INDIRECT OBJECT to people in need]). The markers are a (subject), i (verb), e (direct object), u (indirect object, to, for), o (emphasis).</p>
<p><em>Compounds</em> - two or more words that together indicate a concept (e.g., yedigu yaltangu miau = striped-big-cat = tiger). When a preceding word is a compound rather than an adjective, you use attach -gu to the end of it.</p>
<p><em>Hyphenated</em> - two words joined together for flavor or fine-grained meaning (e.g., ilofun-vardei = to hesitant-look).</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>1. Register and Formality</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Oravia has a natural formal-to-informal spectrum. Register is expressed through choices across four dimensions simultaneously.</p>
<h4>1.1 The Spectrum</h4>
<table>
<tr><th>Feature</th><th>Formal</th><th>Informal</th></tr>
<tr><td>Markers</td><td>All explicit: a, i, e, u</td><td>a drops first, then e; u rarely drops</td></tr>
<tr><td>Word forms</td><td>Full forms: anidai, anifou, yalen</td><td>Short forms: dai, fou, len</td></tr>
<tr><td>Sentences</td><td>Complete with all elements</td><td>Fragments acceptable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Vocabulary</td><td>Core words and compounds</td><td>Created words</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>1.2 Dropping Markers</h4>
<p>The a (subject) marker drops most easily, especially with pronouns. The e (direct object) marker drops when the object is unambiguous. The u (indirect object) marker is rarely dropped even in casual speech.</p>
<pre>a nim i anidai i bonfene → very formal
nim i anidai i bonfene 
i anidai i bonfene 
i dai i bonfene → conversational / casual
i dai i fene 
dai fene → rough speech</pre>
<h4>1.3 Short vs. Long Verb Forms</h4>
<p>When stacking verbs, short forms for the first verb are the natural choice in speech. They are formed by dropping the (sub)cluster and using the root. Using full forms for the first verb sounds formal, deliberate, or written.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Full form</th><th>Short form</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>anidai</td><td>dai</td><td>want to</td></tr>
<tr><td>anifou</td><td>fou</td><td>need to</td></tr>
<tr><td>novil</td><td>vil</td><td>must</td></tr>
<tr><td>daetane</td><td>tane</td><td>should</td></tr>
<tr><td>ando</td><td>do</td><td>can</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>1.4 Affectionate Short Forms</h4>
<p>Doubling the short root creates affectionate or childlike diminutives. The pattern applies to any stackable verb.</p>
<pre>daidai → want (trying to sound cute) / gimme (childlike)
tornono → dear grandchild (from fatorno = grandchild)</pre>
<p>These forms signal a specific social register of intimacy or playfulness.</p>
</div>
</details>

<summary><strong>2. Discourse Framing Phrases</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Several Oravia words function naturally as sentence-initial framing phrases, analogous to "in fact," "for example," and "however" in English. These are familiar words used in a framing position. In writing, they are typically followed by a comma. Here are some of the most common:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Phrase</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Built from</th></tr>
<tr><td>elireva,</td><td>in fact / truly</td><td>eli (virtue) + reva (accurate)</td></tr>
<tr><td>eta,</td><td>therefore / consequently</td><td>eta (therefore)</td></tr>
<tr><td>mai,</td><td>however / on the other hand</td><td>mai (but)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noniu fel,</td><td>for example</td><td>noniu (some) + fel (instance)</td></tr>
<tr><td>neloa lirul,</td><td>always / in every case</td><td>neloa (all) + lirul (habit)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noniu lirul,</td><td>sometimes / in some cases</td><td>noniu (some) + lirul (habit)</td></tr>
<tr><td>yadetu,</td><td>finally</td><td>yadetu (finally)</td></tr>
<tr><td>lar,</td><td>once / at some point (may be story-opening)</td><td>lar (once / past framing marker)</td></tr>
<tr><td>lis,</td><td>one day / at some point in future</td><td>lis (future framing marker)</td></tr>
<tr><td>o iliciu,</td><td>imagine / hypothetically</td><td>o (focus) + iliciu (counterfactual)</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi yuba,</td><td>fortunately</td><td>bi (speaker comment) + yuba (good)</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi tohpu,</td><td>unfortunately</td><td>bi + tohpu (sadness)</td></tr>
<tr><td>yawol,</td><td>generally, broadly</td><td>yawol (broad)</td></tr>
<tr><td>ciugai,</td><td>specifically</td><td>ciugai (detail)</td></tr>
<tr><td>ganyu,</td><td>commonly</td><td>ganyu (average)</td></tr>
<tr><td>yunitam,</td><td>with the exception</td><td>yunitam (special)</td></tr>
<tr><td>nesunya,</td><td>no way, impossible, none</td><td>ne (quantifier) + sunya (zero)</td></tr>
<tr><td>o noi,</td><td>indeed, really</td><td>o (emphasis) + noi (this)</td></tr>
<tr><td>daeyar,</td><td>evidently, obviously</td><td>daeyar (obvious)</td></tr>
<tr><td>geloa,</td><td>in sum</td><td>geloa (sum)</td></tr>
<tr><td>daeta,</td><td>in conclusion</td><td>daeta (conclusion)</td></tr>
<tr><td>tonan,</td><td>suddenly</td><td>tonan (sudden)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noder... mai</td><td>even though / despite</td><td>noder (even) in one sentence and mai (but) in the other</td></tr>
<tr><td>noder</td><td>still, even</td><td>noder (even)</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi ilotanu,</td><td>unexpectedly</td><td>ilotanu (surprise)</td></tr>
<tr><td>domvio,</td><td>instead</td><td>dom (place) + vio (change)</td></tr>
<tr><td>de rodel x,</td><td>according to x,</td><td>de (from) + rodel (worldview)</td></tr>
</table>
<p>You can generally use any word as framing. You can also vary when to use "bi" or "o" preceding the word. These phrases sit most naturally in sentence-initial position but can move freely as modifier blocks.</p>
<pre>lar, hay i elemi en nordau dom. → Once, she lived far away.
bi tohpu, i doum i anocari. → Unfortunately, I cannot leave.
noniu fel, a mogali i domvio e moulu. → For example, coffee replaced milk.</pre>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>3. Contrastive and Ironic Use of o</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>The focus marker o spotlights whatever immediately follows it. Moving o to different positions in the same sentence creates different meanings.</p>
<h4>3.1 Shifting Emphasis</h4>
<p>The same sentence reads differently depending on where o falls:</p>
<pre>nim i moum e mocen → I don't eat chocolate (neutral negation)
o nim i moum e mocen → it's specifically ME who doesn't eat chocolate
nim o i moum e mocen → I really don't eat chocolate
nim i moum o e mocen → I don't eat this chocolate</pre>
<h4>3.2 Corrections</h4>
<p>When pushing back on a claim, place o before the element being corrected to make the correction precise:</p>
<pre>A: i ilaluan ca hay i anocari. → I said that she left.
B: um, o nim i anocari. → No, it's ME who left.</pre>
<h4>3.3 Irony and Sarcasm</h4>
<p>Placing o before a word the listener expects to be sincere, in a context that undercuts it, reads as sarcasm or pointed commentary. The spotlight intensifies the word, and when content doesn't match register, the gap is the meaning:</p>
<pre>o yuba. → How... good. (flat intonation: clearly not good)
o nim eofa. → My 'friend.' (air-quotes via o)</pre>
<p>You can explicitly indicate sarcasm with "bi [yu]parde", which is especially useful in text or social media. But naturally, making sarcasm explicit can ruin the effect.</p>
<p>Irony depends on delivery and context. o is the primary structural tool, but it works only when the surrounding content creates the incongruity.</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>4. Performative Speech — bi el</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>A performative speech act is one where saying the thing IS doing the thing. Oravia marks this with bi el, extending the existing bi stance system.</p>
<h4>4.1 The bi Stance System</h4>
<p>bi already covers a range of speaker stances. bi el adds the performative stance:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Phrase</th><th>Built from</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>bi eosus</td><td>bi + eosus (social hearing)</td><td>I heard this, reported evidence</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi daeniu</td><td>bi + daeniu (witness)</td><td>I witnessed this, direct evidence</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi daeta</td><td>bi + daeta (inference)</td><td>I inferred this, evidential reasoning</td></tr>
<tr><td>bi el</td><td>bi + el (virtue / enactment)</td><td>I hereby enact this — performative</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>4.2 Usage</h4>
<p>bi el sits clause-initially for the strongest performative reading. The accompanying language is usually formal.</p>
<pre>bi el, a nim i elodao. → I hereby promise.
bi el, a nim i eloan u run. → I hereby thank you. (formal gratitude)
bi el, a nim i elarul e ciusiu. → I hereby declare this document official.
bi el, a nim a run faibor. → I hereby become your spouse.</pre>
<p>Clause-final bi el is also possible but reads as a confirmatory tag — "I said it and I mean it" — rather than a framing stance:</p>
<pre>nim i elodao, bi el. → I promise, and I mean it as a binding act.</pre>
<h4>4.3 bi ela — The Ritual Variant</h4>
<p>bi ela draws from the ELA (religious/ritual) subcluster and reads more religious. It is usually reserved for ceremonial or sacred contexts like prayers, blessings, dedications, and oaths.</p>
<pre>bi ela, nim i elano. → This is my prayer. / I hereby pray.
bi ela, nim i elaren e haya. → I hereby bless them.</pre>
<p>For most purposes, bi el is the right choice. bi ela adds specifically sacred register weight if you want it.</p>
<h4>4.4 What bi el is not</h4>
<p>bi el shifts the speech act entirely:</p>
<pre>nim i elodao. → I promise. (statement of intent, which can be revised)
bi el, nim i elodao. → I hereby promise. (the promise itself — enacted by speaking)</pre>
<p>The first can be doubted or deferred. The second cannot, because the utterance IS the act.</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>5. Hyphens</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Any quality word can be hyphenated directly to a verb, coloring the manner in which the action is performed.</p>
<h4>5.1 The Pattern</h4>
<p>The modifier comes first, hyphenated to the main action verb:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Word</th><th>Built from</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>tohpu-anvu</td><td>tohpu (grief) + anvu (go/move)</td><td>to trudge / to move with sadness</td></tr>
<tr><td>aela-ilaluan</td><td>aela (joy) + ilaluan (speak)</td><td>to exclaim happily / to say with joy</td></tr>
<tr><td>tohvor-anifi</td><td>tohvor (fear) + anifi (arrive)</td><td>to approach anxiously / to come with fear</td></tr>
<tr><td>tohpu-bonfene</td><td>tohpu (sadness) + bonfene (lie down)</td><td>to collapse with grief</td></tr>
<tr><td>oipoh-anvu</td><td>oipoh (excitement) + anvu (go)</td><td>to rush / to bound forward with excitement</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>5.2 How This Differs from Other Constructions</h4>
<p>Three ways to connect emotion and action, each with different meaning:</p>
<pre>nim i tohpu-anvu. → I trudge. (the sadness is in the walking itself)
nim non tohpu i anvu. → I walk in a sad manner. (explicit adverb)
nim i anvu, bi tohpu. → I walk, and I'm sad. (speaker comment, parallel)</pre>
<p>The hyphenated form is the most compact and most embodied. It is the choice for literary writing, character description, and situations where the feeling is inseparable from the doing.</p>
<h4>5.3 Extending the Pattern</h4>
<p>Almost any word can precede any action verb.</p>
<pre>yunro-ilaluan → to speak wisely / to say with intelligence
elonor-boemo → to cook with peace 
yadetu-anvu → to move fatalistically</pre>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>6. Cluster as Register</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Because every Oravia word's opening sound signals its semantic cluster, a speaker can layer meaning by choosing a cluster that carries an evaluative charge. Two productive patterns use this for register elevation and register corruption.</p>
<h4>6.1 ti- — The Corrupt Version</h4>
<p>Prepending ti- (from the TI cluster: harm/damage) to any word from another cluster produces the corrupt, wrong, or harmful version of that concept. The original word's meaning is retained but contaminated:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Word</th><th>Original meaning</th><th>ti- form</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>elonor</td><td>peace</td><td>tielonor</td><td>false peace / coerced calm</td></tr>
<tr><td>elumio</td><td>help / aid</td><td>tielumio</td><td>harmful help / aid that harms</td></tr>
<tr><td>roena</td><td>education</td><td>tiroena</td><td>miseducation / indoctrination</td></tr>
<tr><td>eofa</td><td>friendship</td><td>tieofa</td><td>false friendship / manipulation</td></tr>
<tr><td>elodao</td><td>promise</td><td>tielodao</td><td>false promise / a vow that conceals harm</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Simple negations use um or ho. ti- words carry a moral charge, they describe things that wear the form of a virtue while enacting harm. ti-elonor is not the absence of peace or the opposite of peace; it is a corrupted version of peace.</p>
<p>This can also apply to pronouns or forms of address. For example, tirun (ti + run, "you") can be used as an insult. Use this carefully, since it is direct and hostile.</p>
<h4>6.2 el- — The Elevated Version</h4>
<p>Prepending el- creates an elevated, profound, or existentially weighted version of any concept:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Word</th><th>Original</th><th>el- form</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>ilaluan</td><td>to speak</td><td>elilaluan</td><td>a profound utterance / to speak with full weight</td></tr>
<tr><td>anvu</td><td>to go</td><td>elanvu</td><td>purposeful or honorable movement</td></tr>
<tr><td>mo</td><td>food / to eat</td><td>elmo</td><td>sacred eating</td></tr>
<tr><td>elemi</td><td>to live</td><td>elelemi</td><td>to live fully</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Using it on an ordinary act elevates it, and parse use carries the most weight.</p>
<p>It can also be used for formality: if you want to emphasize the person you are speaking to is very noble or elevated, you can attach el to the verbs or nouns that refer to them:</p>
<pre>a run a elraitar? → are you the ruler (elevated)?
i anona u eleoren → I give it to Your Majesty/Your Highness, using el (elevated) + eoren (sir/madam)</pre>
<p>Some people use Elhay or variations to refer to the divine.</p>
<h4>6.3 Combination</h4>
<p>ti- and ele- can apply to the same base word to create a sharp conceptual contrast:</p>
<pre>elumio / tielumio / elelumio → help / harmful or insincere help / sacred service</pre>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>7. Cluster as Irony and Dissonance</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Because clusters carry semantic fields that listeners internalize through use, placing a word into an unexpected cluster creates a felt incongruity: the content says one thing while the register says another. This is a structural way to signal irony, alienation, or emotional distance without stating it directly.</p>
<h4>7.1 How It Works</h4>
<p>In speech, a word from the wrong cluster is marked by its unexpected opening sound. Listeners familiar with the cluster map feel the mismatch even before parsing the meaning. In writing, introduce the coined compound with a definition and let the resonance accumulate through repetition.</p>
<h4>7.2 Example: Love in a Cold Register</h4>
<p>The EL cluster (virtue/wonder) and EO cluster (social) are the natural homes for love, care, and connection. Expressing the same concept through a SE (machine) or JA (tools) cluster word creates dissonance, with different flavors:</p>
<pre>eleyel → love (EL cluster: warm, natural)
'seleyel → robotic, cold (SE cluster: machine )
'jaeleyel → instrumental love (JA cluster: tool)</pre>
<p>You can also combine the cluster with the root, creating the forms 'seyel and 'jayel. Remember created words take ' and may not be accessible to all proficiency levels (§16).</p>
<h4>7.3 Example: Kindness in a Finance Register</h4>
<pre>elumio → help / kindness (EL cluster — genuine)
'gelumio → kindness as leverage</pre>
<h4>7.4 When to Use It</h4>
<p>— Some example of when to use it: a character uses it consistently to reveal worldview (§8); a narrator uses it ironically to contrast with a character's self-description or behavior; or general flavoring for descriptions. </p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>8. Character Voice and Idiolect</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Because every word signals its cluster, consistent cluster preferences across a character's speech create a distinctive voice without requiring new vocabulary. A character who habitually draws from the DA (evaluation) cluster reads as analytical; one who draws from LE (environment) and VA (body) reads as embodied.</p>
<h4>8.1 Cluster Profile Examples</h4>
<table>
<tr><th>Register</th><th>Clusters</th><th>Feel</th></tr>
<tr><td>Analytical</td><td>DA (evaluation), IL (inquiry/cognition), SE (machines/systems)</td><td>Precise, detached, systematic</td></tr>
<tr><td>Embodied</td><td>VA (body vitals), MI/MU (animals), YA (objective qualities)</td><td>Sensorial, grounded</td></tr>
<tr><td>Emotional / social</td><td>TO (suffering), EO (social), frequent bi phrases</td><td>Feeling-forward, relational, vulnerable</td></tr>
<tr><td>Formal / institutional</td><td>DO (regulations), RA (society/governance), RO (knowledge)</td><td>Distant, authoritative, rule-bound</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mythic / poetic</td><td>EL (wonder), HE (fiction tropes), LU (weather/atmosphere)</td><td>Elevated, symbolic, timeless</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>8.2 Applying This in Practice</h4>
<p>Characters describing the same event (a departure) draw on different clusters:</p>
<p>Analytical character:</p>
<pre>hay i anocari caora i daeta ca selota. a nim a 'ilpu. → She left because she assessed it was the fix [for the situation]. I am mind-sad.</pre>
<p>Embodied character:</p>
<pre>hay i ano-anvu. a varodu a 'yacan. → She went away. My head is heavy.</pre>
<p>Emotional character:</p>
<pre>bi tohpu. hay i anocari. a nim a anlaro-toh. → How unfortunate. She left. I am still suffering.</pre>
<p>Same event, three voices. The cluster pattern accumulates across a text into something recognizable as a style.</p>
<h4>8.3 Cluster Drift as Character Development</h4>
<p>A character who begins in one cluster profile and gradually draws from another is changing. A formal character who starts using bi phrases is softening. An emotional character who shifts toward DA (evaluation) is distancing. Cluster drift is a way to show change without stating it.</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>9. Names and Cluster Identity</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>A name can be prefixed with a cluster sound to situate it within a semantic field, adding connotative weight without altering the name itself. The prefix is written lowercase, attached directly to a capitalized name.</p>
<h4>9.1 The Pattern</h4>
<pre>luBia → Bia of the atmosphere 
elBia → Bia of virtue and wonder 
toBia → Bia of suffering / burdened Bia
roBia → Bia of knowledge / scholarly Bia
geBia → Bia of the economy</pre>
<p>The prefix adds connotation and the name remains the name.</p>
<h4>9.2 Ironic and Tragic Name Prefixes</h4>
<p>A character introduced as elBia (virtuous) who is revealed to be corrupt creates a specific irony: the name becomes its own condemnation. A character whose name prefix conflicts with their actual nature, luMaran (atmospheric, shifting) who is in fact rigid and fixed, creates a dissonance that accumulates quietly over a text.</p>
<h4>9.3 Place Names and Coined Concepts</h4>
<p>The same pattern applies to places and invented concepts:</p>
<pre>ti-Babel → corrupt Babel 
ele-Anvu → the Great Journey / the purposeful going (mythic name or concept)</pre>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>10. Coined Compounds</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Oravia's compound system is fully productive: any two cluster words can be crossed to produce a new concept. These coined compounds are author-specific coinings that must be defined at first use and are available for repeated use within a text.</p>
<h4>10.1 The Pattern</h4>
<p>You can coin new words by themselves, or use hyphens.</p>
<pre>'gelonor → economy-peace: the peace of sufficiency, not absence of conflict but the calm of having enough
'rotoh → intellect-suffering: the feeling of understanding something painful, or not being able to comprehend enough
tiohu-elemi → death-life: transformation through ending, a complete circle
lu-ilmava → weather-consciousness: situatedness; how external conditions shape inner awareness</pre>
<h4>10.2 How to Introduce One</h4>
<p>Define at first use. After that, use the compound alone.</p>
<pre>hay i none e 'gelonor, elonor de norfih. → She has "gelonor", the peace from sufficiency.
notor, i do i vardei e hay 'gelonor. → Later, I could see her gelonor.</pre>
<p>These do not become core vocabulary, each person will produce different coinings.</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>12. Rhyme and Root Drift</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Oravia's cluster system creates two specific opportunities for wordplay: rhyming across clusters (same root sound, different domain) and root drift across subclusters (same root sound, shifting meaning through neighboring subclusters).</p>
<h4>12.1 Rhyming Across Clusters</h4>
<p>Two words share the same root but differ in their opening cluster. The rhyme creates a sound bridge between semantic domains, inviting the listener to feel the analogy. For example, in the first sentence of a poem the writer uses "elonor", or virtue-path. By the time we are mid-stanza, it has shifted to "tinor", or "harm-path.</p>
<h4>12.2 Root Drift Across Subclusters</h4>
<p>Within a single cluster, subclusters refine and redirect the domain. Using the same root sound across neighboring subclusters traces movement or change without stating it:</p>
<pre>hay i anifi, i anelem, i anocari. → she arrived, she stilled, she departed (full arc through AN subclusters: ANI → ANE → ANO)</pre>
<p>The three verbs share the AN root. Their subcluster's phonetic and semantic drift from i → e → o mirrors the narrative arc.</p>
<p>This technique works for:</p>
<p>— Escalation: moving through subclusters toward increasing intensity</p>
<p>— Fading: moving toward subclusters of diminishment or rest</p>
<p>— Circling: returning to the opening subcluster to close a loop</p>
<h4>12.3 Affectionate Doubling as Wordplay</h4>
<p>Doubling a short root (§1.4) performs the feeling it names. daidai doesn't just mean want — it sounds like wanting, twice over. In a context where it is unexpected (a formal speech that ends daidai fene), the mismatch creates warmth, humor, or both.</p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>13. Word-Building</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<h4>13.1 How the Cluster System Works</h4>
<p>Every Oravia word begins with a sound (or sound sequence) that signals its semantic cluster. The cluster sound is the first building block of the word, and all words starting with that sound will be conceptually related.</p>
<p>It creates a predictable map: once you know the ~48 cluster sounds, you have a framework for the entire vocabulary. Even an unknown word gives you a domain just from its first syllable.</p>
<pre>mi___ = larger animals
 mihie = horse
 mibeh = goat
 mimu = cow
 miogar = bear (MIO subcluster: wild animals)
mo___ = food / eating
 mogali = coffee
 moaria = apple
 moulu = milk
 boemo = kitchen (BO+E+MO house-room-food)</pre>
<p>Subclusters refine the domain. The third letter (or first two letters of the second syllable) narrows within the cluster:</p>
<pre>AN (action, movement)
 ANE = static actions (rest, stop, stay)
 ANI = movement toward (arrive, bring, attract)
 ANO = movement away (leave, push, send)</pre>
<h4>13.2 How Cross-Cluster Roots Work</h4>
<p>Some roots appear across clusters and add a consistent meaning wherever they appear. The building blocks make Oravia highly guessable. Here are some examples:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Root</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Examples</th></tr>
<tr><td>mir</td><td>many / lots of</td><td>yamirli (old = many-time quality), mir miau (many cats)</td></tr>
<tr><td>yar</td><td>light / radiant</td><td>luyar (lamp/light), oiyar (to emphasize)</td></tr>
<tr><td>non</td><td>way / path / manner</td><td>jenon (path), canon (how/in what way)</td></tr>
<tr><td>tam</td><td>one / single / first</td><td>litam (day = time-1), yunitam (special)</td></tr>
<tr><td>li</td><td>time</td><td>lirul (habit), noli (now)</td></tr>
<tr><td>dom</td><td>place / location</td><td>cedom (where?), ledom (land)</td></tr>
<tr><td>ei</td><td>person</td><td>cei (who?), caei (who), ilhei (person)</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Recognizing these cross-cluster roots lets you parse unfamiliar words without a dictionary. For example, even without knowing elirevaum as a word, a speaker who knows eli (virtue), reva (accurate), and -um (negation) can derive its meaning.</p>
<h4>13.3 The -gu Compound Suffix</h4>
<p>Adding -gu to a modifying word bonds it to the following noun as a single concept rather than a description. This is the primary tool for creating new vocabulary without adding new words.</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Phrase</th><th>Reading</th><th>Contrast</th></tr>
<tr><td>yaltan miau</td><td>a big cat (any big cat, descriptive)</td><td>no -gu: a quality, not a type</td></tr>
<tr><td>yaltangu miau</td><td>big-cat as a type = lion or tiger</td><td>-gu makes it a concept</td></tr>
<tr><td>yedigu yaltangu miau</td><td>striped-big-cat = tiger specifically</td><td>further specification of the type</td></tr>
<tr><td>nordaugu vardei ja</td><td>far-eye tool = binoculars</td><td>three words become one object</td></tr>
<tr><td>yahlulgu yaltangu apafene</td><td>soft-big-seat = couch (as concept)</td><td>vs yahlul yaltan apafene (a descriptive big soft seat)</td></tr>
</table>
<p>Compounds are not exact science. When a compound could be ambiguous, add more words or rephrase.</p>
<h4>13.4 Hyphenated Combinations</h4>
<p>Combine roots freely. The hyphen signals is different from the compound because its main function is not to describe a single concept using multiple words. Instead, it is giving flavor and color to the second word.</p>
<pre>i ilofun-vardei → to hesitant-look, to peek with doubt
i toului-asfe → to tired-stop, to give up
i raidana-ilaluan → to surrender-say, to concede
i tosrei-neiden → to bitter-develop, to feed into your bitterness</pre>
<p>You can also use hyphens to achieve more fine-grained meanings, blending two experiential flavors together. This is commonly done with emotions or abstract nouns:</p>
<pre>aela-tohlel → joy + longing, a type of nostalgic happiness
tohpu-oipoh → sadness + excitement, a type of bittersweetness, holding loss and anticipation at once
mirli-eliyuli → much time + wisdom, understanding that comes with time</pre>
<h4>13.5 Formation Patterns</h4>
<p>The main patterns:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Pattern</th><th>Example</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>cluster + root (most common!)</td><td>yalen (YA+LEN)</td><td>long (objective quality + long)</td></tr>
<tr><td>cluster + cluster</td><td>boemo (BOE+MO)</td><td>kitchen (house room + food)</td></tr>
<tr><td>root + root (least common)</td><td>domvio (DOM+VIO)</td><td>instead, in the place of (place + change)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noun/adjective-gu + noun (compound, forming a concept)</td><td>"yedigu yaltangu miau"</td><td>tiger (striped-big-cat as concept; in this case one can also say the scientific name panthera tigris)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noun/adjective + verb (hyphenated)</td><td>toh-vanvu</td><td>trudge (emotional suffering + walk)</td></tr>
<tr><td>noun + noun, verb + verb (coloring the second word)</td><td>eola-tohlel</td><td>happy-longing (a kind of nostalgic joy)</td></tr>
<tr><td>cluster prefix + word</td><td>tielonor</td><td>corrupted peace (can also say just "tinor", may be more opaque than using both clusters)</td></tr>
<tr><td>ne + number + noun/adjective</td><td>neauta yasoi</td><td>swift (8/10 fast)</td></tr>
</table>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>14. Any Word as a Verb</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>The i marker turns any word into a verb. The most common meanings are covered in the grammar, like these ones:</p>
<pre>i boemo → to cook (kitchen → to do the kitchen thing)
i bonfene → to lie down (bed → to do the bed thing)
i bortal → to enter (door → to do the door thing)
i mogali → to drink coffee (coffee → to do the coffee thing)</pre>
<p>But in some cases, the language leaves the meaning deliberately open. When you want to use a general form, be it in poetry, for style, or as a vague implication, using these words as verb is the right choice. When precision matters, other constructions are available (see grammar for more details).</p>
<p>The words that are deliberately open are usually locations, adjectives, states or abstract words. For example, if you say i bo or i tohpu, what does it mean?</p>
<p>bo — house</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Oravia examples</th><th>Literal gloss</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>hay en bo</td><td>she LOC house</td><td>she is in the house (static location)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay jetai bo</td><td>she DIR house</td><td>she goes toward the house (direction)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i bo-anvu</td><td>she V house-goes</td><td>she goes to the house</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i bortal en bo</td><td>she V enter LOC house</td><td>she enters the house</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i bo-bortal</td><td>she V house-enters</td><td>she enters the house</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i bo e falen</td><td>she V house OBJ kid</td><td>she houses/shelters the kid</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i bo</td><td>she V house</td><td>she houses — open / poetic / vague</td></tr>
</table>
<p>tohpu — sadness</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Oravia examples</th><th>Literal gloss</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>a hay a tohpu</td><td>SUBJ she COPULA sad</td><td>she is sad (state, copula)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i davio no tohpu</td><td>she V become COMPL sad</td><td>she becomes sad (change of state, explicit)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i tohpu-davio</td><td>she V sad-becomes</td><td>she sad-becomes (hyphenation, arrival at state)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i ciudon e tohpu</td><td>she V shows OBJ sad</td><td>she shows sadness (expression, external)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i tohpu-anye</td><td>she V sad-acts</td><td>she acts sad (hyphenation)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i tohpu e x</td><td>she V sad OBJ x</td><td>she causes sadness to x (causative via direct object)</td></tr>
<tr><td>hay i tohpu</td><td>she V sad</td><td>she sads — open / poetic / vague</td></tr>
</table>
<p>The yellow rows mark the bare verb form: the most open reading. All the other rows specify the relationship precisely. As with other matters of style, the choice depends on how you want to express yourself or mold your characters.</p>
</div>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>15. Negation Scope</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>The -um suffix attaches directly to the word it negates. In a simple verb phrase, -um on the verb negates the action. In compound noun phrases, -um can attach to any element within the phrase, creating different scopes. The position of -um determines exactly what is negated while leaving everything else intact.</p>
<h4>15.1 Negating different parts</h4>
<p>Compare readings of the same core elements (mouje = drink, mocen = chocolate, moulu = milk):</p>
<pre>i mouje e mocen moulu → I drink chocolate milk
i moujeum e mocen moulu → I don't drink chocolate milk (whole sentence negated)
i mouje e mocenum moulu → I drink milk but it's not chocolate
i mouje e mocen moulum → I drink something, but it's not chocolate milk</pre>
<h4>15.2 Negation vs. Opposite</h4>
<p>-um negates a quality; ho inverts it. They produce different meanings and different implications:</p>
<pre>a nim a yamirli → I am old
a nim a yamirlium → I am not-old (unspecified other state — not necessarily young)
a nim a ho yamirli → I am young (the explicit opposite)</pre>
</div>
</details>

<details class="gr-part">
<summary><strong>16. Changing Clusters</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>Because almost every Oravia word is built from a (sub)cluster plus a root, a speaker can isolate the root and reattach it to a different cluster. The new word keeps the same core meaning but shifts the emphasis to match the new cluster's domain.</p>
<p>Example: bogor — to build</p>
<p>bogor belongs to the BOE cluster (house spaces, structure). Keeping the root -gor and changing the cluster produces different flavors of the same action:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Example</th><th>Cluster</th><th>Emphasis</th></tr>
<tr><td>bogor</td><td>BOE (house/structure)</td><td>to build (the base form, structural emphasis)</td></tr>
<tr><td>'omgor</td><td>OM (music/sound)</td><td>to build with emphasis on the sound of it</td></tr>
<tr><td>'angor</td><td>AN (action/movement)</td><td>to build with emphasis on the act itself</td></tr>
<tr><td>'togor</td><td>TO (suffering/physical strain)</td><td>to build with emphasis on the physical exertion</td></tr>
<tr><td>'ceigor</td><td>CIU (creative products)</td><td>to build with emphasis on the creativity involved</td></tr>
</table>
<p>All five words describe building. What changes is which aspect of building is being foregrounded. The listener parses the emphasis from the cluster sound alone, without new vocabulary.</p>
<h4>How It Works</h4>
<p>Identify the cluster prefix and the root of a word. Keep the root. Substitute the cluster prefix with one whose domain matches the aspect you want to emphasize.</p>
<p>This technique is available for any word, requires no new vocabulary, and the meaning is recoverable from cluster logic alone. Also, remember to add ' before any word that is not a core word (new coinages).</p>
</div>
</details>

<summary><strong>17. Sample Poem</strong></summary>
<div class="gr-content">
<p>The following poem was written in Oravia to demonstrate how the language's cluster system, word order, and sound patterns can be used as literary devices. Notes and commentary follow.</p>
<p><em>Cea: A Elodao a Yamirli no Li</em></p>
<p>lunu yadetu i iloi a lusari
i om su i apasar 'anesar anelem
notor luval notam i yosari
su i garel a micea e varodu en lufu hem

nim i iloi e lunu yadetu, i siyal
i oi su i apasar 'anesar anelem
notam luval notor val i 'al
su noder micea en noi a lunhem</p>
<h4>Tips</h4>
<table>
<tr><th>Oravia</th><th>English</th></tr>
<tr><td>anelem</td><td>remain, stay</td></tr>
<tr><td>apasar</td><td>hang, suspend</td></tr>
<tr><td>garel</td><td>rise, lift</td></tr>
<tr><td>iloi</td><td>hope, await</td></tr>
<tr><td>lufu</td><td>wind</td></tr>
<tr><td>lunhem</td><td>summer</td></tr>
<tr><td>lunu</td><td>winter</td></tr>
<tr><td>lusari</td><td>sun</td></tr>
<tr><td>luval</td><td>snow</td></tr>
<tr><td>micea</td><td>rabbit, shy</td></tr>
<tr><td>noder</td><td>still, yet</td></tr>
<tr><td>notam</td><td>first</td></tr>
<tr><td>notor</td><td>second</td></tr>
<tr><td>no</td><td>like, similar to</td></tr>
<tr><td>oi</td><td>fun, joy, entertainment cluster</td></tr>
<tr><td>om</td><td>sound</td></tr>
<tr><td>siyal</td><td>find, notice</td></tr>
<tr><td>su</td><td>and, with</td></tr>
<tr><td>val</td><td>ice</td></tr>
<tr><td>varodu</td><td>head</td></tr>
<tr><td>yadetu</td><td>end, final</td></tr>
<tr><td>yosari</td><td>yellow</td></tr>
</table>
<h4>Possible Translation</h4>
<pre>Winter's end, the sun hopes
it resonates and hangs, settles, remains
before the snow, it yellows
and a rabbit lifts its head in the windy heat

Winter's end, I hope, discover
my spirit lifts and I hang, settle, remain
after the snow the now w (the ice melts)
and still the summer here is rabbit (shy)</pre>
<h4>Commentary</h4>
<pre>notam luval notor val i 'al</pre>
<p>I gradually change the word to indicate that the ice is melting. luval (snow) becomes just the root val (ice), which then becomes al. The changing forms and sounds are embodying are melting themselves.</p>
<pre>i apasar 'anesar anelem</pre>
<p>To indicate a kind of settling in anticipation/suspension, I use the words three times inside the same marker (i = verb), which reads as something without pause, while they gradually shift into one another.</p>
<pre>su i garel a micea e varodu en lufu hem
su noder micea en noi a lunhem</pre>
<p>In the last sentence of each stanza, I use the word "micea", but in two different meanings. The first one means literally a rabbit, while the second one means acting shy like a rabbit.</p>
<pre>A Elodao a Yamirli no El</pre>
<p>The alternative title means "A Promise as Old as Wonder", but notice that Elodao has El inside of it. This is because the word "elodao" belongs to the "wonder" cluster (words in Oravia belong to a cluster/classification, indicated by the first syllable). So it means something like the word "promise" returning to its own essence, "el".</p>
<pre>lusari, yosari, hem, lunhem</pre>
<p>Here I use the same roots under different clusters. lusari being "(atmospheric) sun", yosari being "yellow (color sun)". hem being "heat", lunhem being "summer (season heat)". The roots stay the same but the words shift. This mirrors the passage of time while things remain the same within change.</p>
<p>All in all, these linguistic devices play with visuals, phonetics and semantics simultaneously to indicate a hanging anticipation, and change while staying the same.</p>
</div>
